Abstract

This dissertation examines how Chilean Música Urbana became relationally organised
through Spotify-visible artist collaborations between 2014 and 2024. It compares two
periods, formation and national consolidation (2014-February 2022) and boom and
international expansion (March 2022-2024), using descriptive social network measures,
weighted collaboration patterns, community detection, and binary exponential random graph
models. The results show that the collaboration network expanded rapidly while becoming
more structurally integrated, that collaboration remained unevenly distributed, and that
communities were differentiated by generation, musical trajectory, country composition, and
organisational position. The dissertation argues that Chilean Música Urbana should be
understood not only as a genre label, but as a relationally organised music world shaped by
platform visibility, generational differentiation, and selective internationalisation.

1. Introduction

Over the last decade, Música Urbana has become one of the most visible and commercially
successful sectors of Latin American popular music (Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025). Artists
associated with the category have accumulated billions of streams, occupied prominent
positions in digital rankings, and expanded their audiences across national borders. In Chile,
this process accelerated during the early 2020s, when artists such as Cris Mj, Polimá
Westcoast, Floyymenor, Pailita, Jere Klein, Paloma Mami and others achieved
unprecedented levels of visibility both nationally and internationally. As a result, Música
Urbana has become a central reference point for artists, audiences, media outlets, streaming
platforms, and music industry actors.

The term Música Urbana, or Género Urbano, is retained in Spanish rather than translated as
urban music in order to preserve its situated meaning within Latin American musical
contexts. In Chile and across the region, Música Urbana functions as an emic category that
encompasses genres such as reggaetón, trap, dembow, mambo chileno, and rap, while also
referring to broader cultural practices, identities, and forms of participation (Muñoz-Tapia et
al., 2025; Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). Although the category is now widely
used, its meaning remains contested. Artists grouped under the label often differ in musical
style, trajectories, audiences, and aesthetic references, raising questions about what connects
them and how such a heterogeneous category acquires social coherence.

Existing research has largely approached this question through the study of genres, cultural
classifications, media discourse, platformisation, and the political economy of digital music
(Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025; Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). These studies have
shown that Música Urbana is neither a stable genre nor a simple continuation of earlier
musical traditions. Rather, it emerged through processes of circulation, translation,
categorisation, and institutional recognition that connected previously distinct musical
practices. Building on Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos (2025), this dissertation takes the
generational and categorical formation of Chilean Música Urbana as a starting point and asks
how that category became relationally organised across time. The central contribution is
therefore not to identify the generational divide itself, already described in previous research,

                                                                                                             5
but to examine how this categorical and generational transformation became organized
through collaboration networks.

This question is especially relevant in the context of streaming. As Hesmondhalgh (2025)
argues, music streaming has produced ambivalent transformations. It has increased the
international visibility of genres, scenes, and markets beyond the historical centres of the
global music industry, particularly the United States and the United Kingdom, contributing
to a more multipolar circulation of music. At the same time, this diversification has not
eliminated structural inequalities. Platform ownership, digital infrastructures, algorithmic
intermediation, and international intellectual property regimes continue to concentrate power
within the global music economy. Chilean Música Urbana is a productive case for examining
this tension: it has contributed to the formation of a relatively distinctive artistic world and
local market, while remaining deeply articulated with transnational genres, international
collaborations, platform-based visibility, and the commercial logics of streaming.

This dissertation argues that collaboration networks provide a useful way of examining these
processes. Collaborations are not merely technical arrangements for producing songs. They
are also mechanisms through which artists gain visibility, access new audiences, establish
reputations, and create connections across musical communities. In this sense, collaboration
networks make it possible to observe how heterogeneous genres and artists become
articulated into broader musical formations. Rather than focusing exclusively on discourse
or classification, this study examines the relationships through which Música Urbana is
enacted in practice.

To do so, the dissertation analyses collaboration networks among artists associated with
Chilean Música Urbana between 2014 and 2024 using data derived from Spotify metadata.
The analysis compares two historical periods: a phase of formation and national
consolidation (2014­February 2022) and a phase of boom and international expansion
(March 2022­2024). Drawing on relational sociology, social network analysis, and research
on music worlds, the study investigates how collaboration patterns changed between these
periods as the scene expanded, became more structurally integrated, and internationalised
selectively rather than uniformly.

                                                                                                             6
The dissertation addresses three research questions. First, how did the structure of
collaboration networks change between the formation and national consolidation period and
the boom and international expansion period? Second, to what extent did processes of
internationalisation transform both the composition and relational structure of collaboration
networks? Third, how were collaboration opportunities distributed among artists as the scene
expanded in scale, visibility, and international reach?

By addressing these questions, the dissertation contributes to research on music worlds,
cultural classification, and digital cultural production. More broadly, it shows how social
network analysis can be used to investigate the relational transformation of musical
categories over time. In this case, the comparison between periods makes it possible to move
from the question of categorical emergence to the question of how Música Urbana expanded,
integrated, and internationalised as a collaboration network.

2. Theoretical Framework

2.1 Categories and Relations: Musical Assemblages, Social Identity,
and Social Network Analysis

Musical genre can be understood as a historically situated category that classifies musical
practices while linking them to particular audiences, identities, institutions, and modes of
circulation. Following Brackett (2016), genres do not possess fixed essences; they acquire
meaning relationally, through their position within changing systems of musical difference.
Brackett develops this argument through a reading of genealogical, citational, and dialogical
approaches associated with Foucault (1977), Derrida (1988), and Bakhtin (1981), while also
building on Fabbri's theory of musical genres as systems of conventions and diachronic
processes (Fabbri, 1982, 2012). From this perspective, genres persist because works repeat,
cite, and transform recognisable conventions over time. Genre is therefore not simply a label
attached to sound, but a historically produced way of organising recognition.

This approach links genre to a conception of assemblage. Brackett (2016) and Drott (2013)
both understand genres as configurations of heterogeneous elements connected through

                                                                                                             7
relations of exteriority: sounds, images, performance styles, modes of dress, forms of speech,
technologies, institutions, markets, media, and classificatory devices. A genre is therefore
not reducible to musical style alone. It is an arrangement of sonic, social, institutional, and
technological elements that can be reconfigured over time. This point is important for the
present dissertation because networks of artist collaboration on songs can be considered part
of a broader set of relations through which musical categories are produced and recognised.

Musical categories also organise forms of identification. Brackett (2016), drawing on Born
(2000, 2011), shows that genres become associated with social groups through historically
contingent processes involving class, race, ethnicity, gender, generation, and market position.
These associations are neither natural nor stable. They are produced through circulation,
reception, media discourse, institutional classification, and artistic practice. Genre categories
therefore help constitute musical publics: they provide names, expectations, and boundaries
through which actors understand themselves and others. In this sense, musical categories are
not merely descriptive; they are performative classifications that shape how cultural
participation becomes socially intelligible.

From another perspective, Social Network Analysis and relational sociology have also
addressed the relationship between categories and relations. White's concept of catnets is
central here. For White (2008), social formations emerge through the intersection of
categorical identities and network ties. Categories allow actors to recognise one another as
belonging to a shared formation, while relations provide the channels through which those
categories circulate, stabilise, and become consequential. This framework is especially useful
for studying musical worlds because artists may share a category without all being directly
connected, and they may become connected in ways that subsequently reshape the meaning
of the category itself.

Building on White and Tilly, Crossley (2025a) develops this point by linking Social Network
Analysis with Social Identity Theory. Social Identity Theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979)
explains how actors construct shared identities through categories, norms, and prototypes,
and how these classifications orient perception, recognition, and affiliation. However, it tends
to under-theorize the relational channels through which identities circulate and acquire
durability. Social Network Analysis, by contrast, explains how homophily, closure,

                                                                                                             8
brokerage, diffusion, and network position structure interaction, but often gives less attention
to the symbolic categories through which actors interpret those relations. Crossley's
contribution is to bring these two perspectives together.

From this perspective, categories and networks are mutually constitutive. Categories
circulate through networks and become socially effective when they are recognized, shared,
and enacted by connected actors. At the same time, categories shape networks by guiding
affiliation, recognition, in-group preference, and expectations about appropriate ties. Actors
do not collaborate only because they occupy positions in a structure; they also interpret one
another through categories that make some collaborations more thinkable, legitimate, or
desirable than others. Conversely, repeated relations can reinforce, modify, or destabilize the
categories through which actors understand the field. This retroactive relation between
categories and ties is central to the dissertation's argument.

Social Network Analysis therefore provides a methodological strategy for examining one
dimension of this process. It does not explain musical categories on its own, nor does it
reduce genres to networks. Its contribution lies in making relational configurations visible:
who collaborates with whom, how ties are distributed, which actors occupy central or
peripheral positions, how clusters form, and whether collaboration follows patterns of
generational proximity, national boundary-making, or categorical similarity. These patterns
require historical and cultural interpretation, but they provide empirical evidence about how
categories become organized through relations.

This is important considering the ambiguity and fluidity of a category such as música urbana.
In that sense, the notion of hypergenre is useful for understanding categories that gather
heterogeneous musical forms without eliminating internal differences. Buch's work on
hypergenre describes broad musical categories that organise diverse practices under a shared
classificatory umbrella (Buch, 2013, 2018). Música Urbana can be approached in these
terms. Rather than naming a bounded genre, it operates as a glocal hypergenre that connects
rap, trap, reggaeton, dembow, mambo chileno, RKT, and other local or transnational variants
(Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025). Its coherence does not depend on a single musical essence, but
on the capacity of the category to connect heterogeneous sounds, actors, scenes, markets, and
infrastructures.

                                                                                                             9
Previous historical and ethnographic research on Chilean Música Urbana has shown that the
category emerged through interactions among artists, media, platforms, audiences, industry
actors, local scenes, and global tendencies (Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025; Muñoz-Tapia and
Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). This earlier work adopted a perspective close to assemblage
approaches by reconstructing the historical formation of Música Urbana as a category and
identifying the meanings, tensions, and divisions that structure the Chilean case. In particular,
it highlighted the importance of generational differentiation between historical rap
trajectories and younger urbano formations. The present dissertation builds on that
interpretation but shifts the empirical focus toward artist collaboration on songs,
operationalised as Spotify-visible co-presence among credited performers on the same track.
It examines how those historically identified divisions are organised relationally, while also
incorporating additional attributes such as internationalisation, gender, artist type, and
record-label position.

In this dissertation, Música Urbana is therefore analysed simultaneously as a category and
as a relational formation. The category provides a historically situated framework of
recognition, while networks of artist collaboration on songs reveal how performers associated
with and around that category became connected across time. This approach allows the
dissertation to examine how Chilean Música Urbana was not only named, narrated, or
classified, but also enacted through credited collaboration. The central question is not
whether Música Urbana is a "real" genre in a bounded stylistic sense, but how it became
socially effective as a category through patterned relations that sustained, expanded, and
differentiated it.

2.2 Collaboration Networks and Music Worlds

Scholars working on music, cultural production, and collaboration have consistently
emphasised that music is a collective activity rather than the product of isolated creators.
Small's (1998) concept of musicking treats music as a relational activity involving
performers, listeners, mediators, and facilitators, while Becker's (2008) theory of art worlds
understands artistic production as a cooperative system coordinated through conventions
among creators, technicians, distributors, critics, and audiences. Farrell's (2001) notion of

                                                                                                            10
collaborative circles similarly highlights how sustained peer interaction can generate
innovation and mutual influence.

Crossley develops this argument through the concept of music worlds (Crossley, 2009, 2015,
2020, 2025b; Crossley et al., 2015). Music worlds are relational formations in which musical
practices are produced, circulated, and sustained. His contribution is both theoretical and
methodological: collaborations, shared spaces, reputations, and recurrent interactions
generate the relational infrastructure through which scenes emerge and endure. Social
network analysis therefore complements sociohistorical interpretation by allowing
researchers to identify the structures through which music worlds acquire coherence.

Research on music networks shows that collaboration is not merely a technical arrangement
for producing songs. It is also a mechanism through which music worlds are formed, scenes
acquire cohesion, and categories become socially credible. Kirschbaum and Carvalho de
Vasconcelos (2007) analyse Tropicalia as the outcome of strategic connections across
previously separated musical communities, while Hammou (2009) shows that French rap
developed as a professional social world through a cohesive network of featuring
collaborations. Similarly, Grubesic et al. (2024) demonstrate that Yacht Rock depended upon
an interconnected network of musicians embedded within the social and spatial infrastructure
of Los Angeles.

Collaboration networks also influence artistic careers and forms of visibility. Network
position may affect artistic success (Silva et al., 2019), increase future streaming visibility
(Deshmane and Martinez-de-Albeniz, 2020), and generate reputational associations among
artists and composers (Braden et al., 2024). Network evolution also matters: Bagley et al.
(2022) identify processes of fragmentation and recombination in musical collaboration
networks, while Xu et al. (2025) show that social ties can expand the range of musical
preferences available to individuals.

A further contribution of this literature concerns the relationship between local and external
ties. Makkonen (2017) argues that local networks generate opportunities and support career
development, whereas external connections are often more important for broader visibility
and international projection. Similarly, Diani and Sacchetti (2023) show that artistic vitality
tends to increase when musicians combine local embeddedness with external connections.

                                                                                                            11
This distinction is central for the present study because the question is not only whether
Chilean Música Urbana expanded, but also whether its collaboration structure became more
internationally connected.

Digitalisation has transformed these relational dynamics. According to Makelberge (2012),
technological changes in production infrastructures modify the conditions of collaboration
by enabling creative interaction beyond shared physical spaces. In contemporary music
worlds, collaborations increasingly occur through mediated environments in which digital
platforms, metadata, and visibility metrics become part of the relational process itself.

The featuring is therefore a particularly useful relation for studying Música Urbana. Unlike
friendship, influence, or informal cooperation, featured collaborations are public, credited,
platform-visible, and cumulative. They create observable traces of association between artists
that can be systematically reconstructed through digital metadata. While featuring
collaborations do not capture the full social life of musical production, they provide a
meaningful relational layer through which patterns of cooperation, recognition, and visibility
become empirically observable.

3. Research Background and Hypothesis Formulation

3.1 From Urban Contemporary to Chilean Música Urbana

The emergence of Música Urbana in Chile forms part of broader transformations in the
categorisation of popular music. One important antecedent is Urban Contemporary, which
emerged in the United States during the 1970s and 1980s as a radio and industry classification
grouping diverse forms of Black popular music (Hammou, 2023; Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025).
A related process occurred within Latin music industries, where labels such as urban, Latin
urban, reggaeton, and Latin rap became associated through shared markets, media circuits,
and cultural imaginaries (Rivera-Rideau, 2015). These categories did not describe unified
musical styles. Rather, they assembled heterogeneous genres through infrastructures of
circulation, audiences, and institutional classification.

                                                                                                            12
This dissertation approaches Música Urbana in Chile as a situated category related to, but
not identical with, those classifications. Categories such as Urban Contemporary, hurban,
Latin Urban, and Música Urbana function as false friends: they appear equivalent because
they share labels, references, and classificatory functions, while in practice organising
different musical worlds and historical trajectories (Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025). At the same
time, they reveal elective affinities among heterogeneous genres such as rap, reggaeton, trap,
dembow, mambo chileno, and RKT. These affinities are not only musical but also social,
since urban categories have frequently grouped cultural expressions associated with
racialised populations, working-class sectors, migrants, Indigenous peoples, and ethnic
minorities. They do not eliminate difference; rather, they create conditions under which
diverse musical practices can be recognised as part of a broader cultural formation.

The Chilean case reflects these broader dynamics while following its own historical
trajectory. Since the late 1980s and early 1990s, Hip Hop scenes established local
infrastructures of production, circulation, and artistic socialisation, contributing to
independent forms of cultural production that later shaped the emergence of urban genres
(Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). Contemporary Música Urbana, however, cannot
be reduced to a continuation of Chilean rap. During the 2010s, digital production
technologies, social media, streaming platforms, and digital distribution services facilitated
the growth of trap, reggaeton, mambo chileno, and related genres among younger
generations. By the early 2020s, Música Urbana had become a widely recognised category
capable of grouping these heterogeneous trajectories under a common label (Muñoz-Tapia
et al., 2025; Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025).

At the same time, Música Urbana maintained a complex relationship with rap. In Chile and
much of Latin America, rap has traditionally been associated with Hip Hop culture, boom
bap aesthetics, and underground scenes (Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). Unlike
the United States, where rap or Hip Hop often function as broader categories that encompass
styles such as trap, Música Urbana in Latin America and Spain emerged as a wider umbrella
that groups rap alongside genres with distinct trajectories, including reggaeton, trap, and
dembow. Within this configuration, reggaeton has played a particularly important role in

                                                                                                            13
articulating connections between otherwise heterogeneous genres and audiences within the
broader metageneric structure of Música Urbana (Arias Salvado, 2025).

Rather than examining this process through discourse alone, this dissertation analyses the
collaboration networks through which these connections were enacted. If Música Urbana
emerged through the articulation of heterogeneous musical traditions, collaboration networks
provide a way to examine how those articulations became structured over time.

3.2 Spotify, Collaboration and Platform Visibility

Spotify occupies a central position in this study because it functions simultaneously as a
platform for music circulation and as a source of relational data. This is particularly relevant
for Chilean Música Urbana, whose expansion has been closely associated with streaming
platforms, social media, digital distributors, and platform-mediated forms of visibility.

This role should be situated within broader processes of digital transformation. A first phase
was characterised by disintermediation, as home studios, MP3 technologies, websites, blogs,
peer-to-peer systems, and social media reduced barriers to recording, distribution, and
promotion, allowing artists to circulate music outside traditional industry structures
(Albornoz, 2011; Buquet, 2003; Fouce, 2012; Gallo and Semán, 2016; Leyshon, 2001). In
Chile, these developments coincided with the consolidation of rap as a largely underground
and independent musical world (Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2023, 2025).

A second phase emerged with the rise of digital streaming platforms. Rather than eliminating
intermediaries, digitalisation created new forms of reintermediation, in which distributors,
playlists, recommendation systems, metrics, algorithms, and major labels became central to
organising visibility and audience reach (Lamacchia, 2016; Leyshon, 2001; Negus, 2019;
Prey, 2016). Spotify is therefore not simply a distribution channel but part of a broader
ecosystem through which artistic visibility and commercial value are organised.

For the purposes of this dissertation, Spotify is important not only as an industry actor but
also as an empirical source. Track-level collaborations constitute public, credited, and
platform-visible relations between artists. Although they do not capture the full range of
creative and organisational relationships involved in music production, they provide

                                                                                                            14
observable traces of artistic association that can be systematically reconstructed through
metadata.

3.3 Periodisation and Hypotheses

This dissertation examines collaboration networks during a period of rapid transformation in
Chilean Música Urbana. To capture these changes, the analysis distinguishes between two
historical phases. The first, Formation and National Consolidation (January 2014­February
2022), corresponds to the emergence and consolidation of Música Urbana as a recognisable
category within Chile. During this period, artists associated with rap, trap, reggaeton, and
related genres increasingly interacted through collaborations, digital platforms, and shared
audiences.

The second phase, Boom and International Expansion (March 2022­December 2024),
corresponds to a period of accelerated growth and transnational visibility. This period was
marked by the international success of Chilean artists, increased presence in global streaming
rankings, growing industry attention, and more collaborations with foreign artists. Songs
such as Una Noche en Medellín1 and Ultra Solo2 became emblematic of this broader
transformation.

The distinction between these periods should be understood as an analytical device rather
than a rigid historical rupture. Processes of internationalisation were already present before
2022, while domestic collaborations remained important afterwards. Nevertheless, the
periodisation captures an important shift in the scale, visibility, and geographical reach of the
scene.

characterised by a greater number of artists and collaborations and by stronger component-

H2. Internationalization

The boom and international expansion period will contain a greater proportion of foreign

H3. Tie Intensification

The boom and international expansion period will contain a higher proportion of recurrent
collaborations, reflecting the consolidation of collaborative relationships within the scene.

H4. Uneven Collaboration Participation

Despite overall expansion, collaborations will remain unevenly distributed, with a minority
of artists accumulating substantially more ties while many artists enter the network through
only one or a few collaboration partners.

These hypotheses treat changes in network structure as indicators of broader transformations
in the organisation of Chilean Música Urbana. The following section describes the data
sources, network construction procedures, and analytical strategies used to evaluate these
expectations.

4. Data and Methods

4.1 Data Sources and Extraction

The collaboration networks were constructed from a larger database of Chilean musicians
and their Spotify-visible collaborations. Data collection combined two complementary
sources. The primary source was the Spotify Web API, accessed through the Spotipy Python
library using Spotify's Client Credentials workflow. The extraction retrieved artist metadata,
releases, tracks, credited performers, and release dates. Historical information on tracks

                                                                                                            16
appearing in the Spotify Top 200 chart for Chile was retrieved from Kworb and used as
complementary evidence of platform visibility and ranking presence.

The Spotify extraction began from manually curated playlists focused on Chilean music,
which were screened to retain lists composed of Chilean artists and used as seed lists for
identifying national performers. From this seed set, the Python pipeline retrieved artist
identifiers and metadata, queried releases, and extracted track-level performer credits.

Collaborations were generated at the track level. Whenever a Spotify track listed more than
one credited performer, all unordered artist pairs among those performers were created. Each
collaboration record therefore contains two Spotify artist identifiers, a Spotify track
identifier, and the release date. The resulting networks capture public, credited co-presence
on Spotify releases rather than songwriting, production, management, friendship, or live-
performance relationships. Because the data indicate joint participation on a track, the
networks are undirected.

The extracted files should be understood as historical source data rather than as a
continuously updated representation of Spotify. Platform metadata can change over time, and
the original API responses were not archived as immutable snapshots. The repository
preserves the extracted collaboration tables and processing logic used in this dissertation, but
it cannot reconstruct later changes made by Spotify to artist profiles, genre labels, release
credits, or follower counts.

4.2 Network Construction and Delimitation

The initial collaboration database spans multiple genres, scenes, and historical trajectories.
Rather than beginning with a fixed list of artists, the network was delimited around a broad
Chilean Música Urbana seed category while preserving relevant ties to adjacent musical
fields and international collaborators. This approach reflects the assumption that Música
Urbana is best studied as a relational, historically situated category rather than a fixed
stylistic genre.

Network delimitation proceeded in three steps. First, Spotify's genre metadata provided an
inclusive automated screening. Artists were initially coded as potential Música Urbana cases

                                                                                                            17
when their genre field contained at least one of the following terms: reggaeton, hip hop, trap,
rap, mambo, urbana, urbano, neoperreo, r&b, guaracha, dancehall, rkt, neo soul, acid jazz, or
dembow. These labels were used only to construct the initial candidate universe, not as
definitive genre categories.

Second, we reviewed the preliminary classification artist by artist. Spotify's platform labels
were not treated as definitive because they inconsistently combine stylistic descriptions,
national scenes, commercial categories, and algorithmically generated associations. Manual
validation drew on previous research on Música Urbana and related musical genealogies in
Chile, together with contextual knowledge of artist trajectories, repertoires, collaborations,
and public positioning. This procedure allowed artists omitted or ambiguously classified by
Spotify to be included while removing false positives generated by the broad keyword search.

Third, the manually validated Chilean Música Urbana seed was expanded one step outward.
To enter the analytic network, Chilean seed artists had to participate in at least one retained
Spotify collaboration released between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024. Tracks
released outside this observation window were not counted. Foreign artists and non-urban
artists were retained whenever they collaborated directly with the Chilean Música Urbana
seed during the same period.

Nodes represent credited performers appearing on Spotify releases and include both solo
artists and artistic groups or collectives. The final analytic network excludes entities that
primarily operate as record labels, imprints, distributors, publishing companies, studios,
booking agencies, or service brands. It also excludes duplicate collective accounts when
including them would reproduce collaborations already represented by individual members.
Before constructing the final networks, the manually validated artist database was restricted
to profiles with more than 1,000 Spotify followers. These exclusions delimited the network;
the subsequent analysis examines how artists classified within and beyond this boundary are
connected through Spotify-visible collaborations.

4.3 Node Attribute Generation

Node attributes were generated through a two-stage process. First, the Spotify Web API
provided artist identifiers and platform metadata, including follower counts, popularity

                                                                                                            18
scores, and Spotify genre labels. Because Spotify does not provide standardised historical or
biographical information, the database was subsequently enriched through an AI-assisted
information retrieval workflow using Google's Gemini Flash 2.0 language model with web
search. This workflow retrieved publicly available information on artist trajectories, places
of origin and artistic formation, debut releases, career milestones, record-label affiliations,
gender, and group membership composition.

The AI-assisted stage was used for retrieval and organisation rather than for final
classification. Each extracted attribute was accompanied by a confidence rating and
supporting sources when available. Final coding decisions were manually reviewed,
corrected, and harmonised before inclusion in the analytical database.

4.4 Variable Operationalisation

The validated information was transformed into analytical variables suitable for network
analysis. The principal node attributes used in the descriptive and modelling analyses are
country, artist type, gender, record-label affiliation, and generation. The broad Música
Urbana classification was used primarily to delimit the analytical network, while the network
analyses examine the relational patterns produced by collaboration ties.

Generation was operationalised from the year of professional musical inception. For
descriptive and community analyses, this year was grouped into cohorts: decade-based
categories for artists beginning before 2000 and five-year intervals from 2000 onward. For
ERGM models, the same inception year was used as a continuous attribute, centred within
each period, in order to estimate the effect of temporal proximity between artists. When the
inception year could not be established, we used the release year of the earliest Spotify track
as a proxy. Record-label affiliation was classified into hierarchical categories distinguishing
major labels, major subsidiaries, independent labels, and cases without an identifiable label.
When artists were affiliated with more than one label type within the same observation
period, the highest-ranking category was retained. For groups and collectives, gender was
operationalised according to membership composition and subsequently harmonised into
broader analytical categories where appropriate.

                                                                                                            19
4.5 Weighted Networks and Periodisation

The networks are initially weighted. For each period, artist identifiers were converted into
canonical unordered pairs, so that (A)-(B) and (B)-(A) represented the same dyad. Track-
level records were then deduplicated using the combination of unordered dyad and Spotify
track identifier. This prevents repeated extraction of the same track from inflating
collaboration frequencies while preserving different tracks credited to the same pair. After
canonicalization and deduplication, the final edge lists contain no self-loops, reverse-
orientation duplicates, or repeated dyads.

The raw weight of a tie equals the number of unique retained Spotify tracks on which two
artists were jointly credited during a given period. Because collaboration frequencies are
strongly right-skewed, raw counts were also recoded into three ordered categories: 1 =
sporadic for one collaboration, 2 = weak recurrent for two collaborations, and 3 =
recurrent/strong for three or more collaborations. This ordinal measure is used in the
descriptive analysis of collaboration intensity, the network visualisations, and the community
detection analysis. It preserves meaningful differences between one-time and recurrent
collaborations while reducing the influence of a small number of highly prolific dyads.

For the ERGM analysis, the networks were further dichotomised. The main ERGM
specification models whether at least one collaboration exists between two artists within a
period. This binary strategy was selected because the valued and ordinal ERGM
specifications were less stable for the dissertation's comparative modelling purposes. The
weighted and ordinal measures are therefore retained for descriptive and community
analyses, while the ERGM focuses on the presence or absence of collaboration ties.

Separate networks were constructed for each historical period. Collaborations were assigned
to periods according to track release date, allowing comparison between the Formation and
National Consolidation period (2014-February 2022) and the Boom and International
Expansion period (March 2022-2024). The two networks are non-cumulative: collaborations
observed in the first period are not automatically carried into the second period. Tracks with
incomplete calendar dates were excluded in order to apply a uniform date-quality rule.

4.7 Analytical Strategy

Although this dissertation relies primarily on quantitative network data, the analytical
strategy is informed by previous historical and ethnographic research on the emergence of
Chilean Música Urbana (Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). That work helps identify
the meanings, categories, and divisions sociologically relevant to interpreting the network
results, especially the generational distinction between historical rap trajectories and younger
urbano formations. In this sense, the study follows a mixed-methods logic rather than a fully
independent mixed-methods design: qualitative knowledge derived from interviews,

                                                                                                            21
participant observation, and historical reconstruction informs the construction and
interpretation of quantitative network evidence (Domínguez and Hollstein, 2011).
All analyses were conducted in R using RStudio. Data cleaning, recoding, and table
construction were carried out mainly with tidyverse packages, including dplyr, tidyr, readr,
and stringr. Network objects and structural measures were produced using igraph, tidygraph,
and network, while visualisations were generated with ggplot2, ggraph, and related plotting
tools. Community detection was implemented through modularity-based algorithms, with
particular attention to the Leiden solution, which was selected for its interpretable and stable
meso-level clusters. ERGM models were estimated using the ergm and statnet framework,
while goodness-of-fit and diagnostic procedures drew on ergm, btergm, and associated
simulation tools. The dissertation was written and rendered with Quarto, allowing text, code,
tables, and figures to be integrated in a reproducible workflow.
The analytical strategy proceeds in three stages. First, descriptive statistics compare the two
period-specific networks in terms of size, density, degree distribution, connected
components, centralisation, transitivity, and weighted collaboration intensity. These
measures establish whether the network expanded, became more integrated, and changed in
the distribution of collaboration activity. Second, community detection identifies meso-level
collaboration clusters and examines how these clusters differ by generation, country
composition, gender, artist type, label affiliation, and collaboration intensity. Third, binary
ERGMs are used to evaluate whether selected structural and attribute-based mechanisms are
associated with the probability of collaboration ties. The ERGM results are interpreted
cautiously, not as causal explanations, but as formal tests of relational patterns suggested by
the descriptive and community analyses.

weighted. Binary transitivity decreased from 0.332 to 0.233, indicating fewer closed triangles
relative to connected triples. In contrast, weighted transitivity increased using both raw
counts and ordinal weights. The network therefore did not become more closed in the binary
sense; rather, the closed local configurations that remained were characterised by greater
collaboration intensity.

                                                                                                            29
5.1.4 Synthesis of Descriptive Findings

Taken together, the descriptive analyses indicate that the Spotify-visible collaboration
network of Chilean Música Urbana underwent a substantial structural transformation
between the two periods. The network grew rapidly in size, but this growth was accompanied
by greater component-level integration rather than fragmentation. Collaboration activity also
became more heterogeneous: most artists continued to have few partners, while a smaller set
accumulated more ties and stronger collaboration intensity. Closure followed a dual pattern:
binary transitivity declined, whereas weighted transitivity increased, indicating stronger
cohesion within the closed configurations that remained. The participating population also
changed, with a larger foreign share, more varied label affiliations, and a greater presence of
artists whose trajectories began in recent years. Overall, the shift from formation and national
consolidation to boom and international expansion involved broader participation, stronger
integration, and persistent local intensity.

These descriptive patterns also inform the following analytical stages. The combination of
lower density, larger connected components, and declining centralisation suggests that
expansion did not simply produce a larger version of the first-period network, but a more
integrated structure that remained heterogeneous. This motivates the community detection
analysis, which examines whether this heterogeneity is organised into meso-level
collaboration clusters. At the same time, the stable median degree, rising maximum degree,
and increasing collaboration intensity indicate that tie formation is unevenly distributed
across artists, while the contrast between declining binary transitivity and increasing
weighted transitivity suggests that local cohesion is associated with stronger recurrent ties.
These patterns also motivate the ERGM strategy, which evaluates whether generational
proximity, Chile-Foreign collaboration, categorical similarity, and low-degree participation
are associated with the presence of collaboration ties across the two periods.

5.2 Community Structure

Community detection examines the meso-level organisation of the Spotify-visible
collaboration network. Whereas the previous section showed that the network expanded and
became more structurally integrated, this section asks how that increasingly connected

                                                                                                            30
collaboration space is internally organised. The analysis bridges descriptive network
statistics and the ERGM models by identifying the main collaboration clusters that require
interpretation before modelling tie formation.

The analysis is restricted to the largest connected component of each period. This makes
community structure comparable across periods and avoids treating disconnected peripheral
groups as substantive communities. Leiden is used as the main specification because it is
designed to identify well-connected communities and is less prone than Louvain to producing
internally disconnected clusters. Louvain is estimated as a robustness check during model
selection. For this reason, the substantive interpretation focuses on the selected Leiden
partitions.

Formally, the selected Leiden solutions optimise weighted modularity. For a partition c,
modularity can be written in simplified form as:

Q = (1 / 2m)  [ w -  (k k / 2m) ] (c, c)
where w is the ordinal collaboration weight between artists i and j, k and k are their
weighted degrees, m is the total edge weight,  is the resolution parameter, and (c, c) equals
one when two artists are assigned to the same community and zero otherwise. In this
dissertation, the procedure identifies groups of artists with more and stronger internal
collaborations than would be expected from their weighted connectivity alone.

5.2.1 Selecting the Community Solution

The number of communities was evaluated rather than fixed in advance. For each period and
method, we estimated repeated partitions across a common grid of resolution parameters and
random seeds. We retained only candidate partitions containing between three and twelve
communities. The selection procedure compared modularity, stability across runs,
community-size balance, and interpretability. The objective was not to maximise modularity
mechanically, but to identify a solution that preserved empirical fit without unnecessary
fragmentation.

The first diagnostic uses the best modularity observed for each value of (k). It shows where
modularity peaks and whether gains flatten as additional communities are introduced.

5.2.4 Historical and Theoretical Interpretation

The Leiden results show that community structure is differentiated by size, collaboration
intensity, nationality, generation, and organisational position. These patterns do not explain
the historical meaning of each community on their own, but they provide a relational map
that can be read in dialogue with the historical and ethnographic reconstruction developed
earlier in the dissertation and in previous work on Chilean Música Urbana (Muñoz-Tapia et
al., 2025; Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). In this sense, community detection helps
assess whether the category's historical formation leaves observable traces in Spotify-visible
collaboration networks.

The communities should therefore not be interpreted as bounded genres. They combine artists
with different profiles, suggesting that collaboration clusters are formed through the
articulation of stylistic, generational, industrial, and transnational relations. This is consistent
with relational and assemblage approaches to genre, which understand musical categories as
historically situated configurations of sounds, actors, institutions, media, and classificatory
practices rather than fixed stylistic containers (Brackett, 2016; Drott, 2013). It also connects
with White's and Crossley's arguments that categories and networks are mutually implicated:
categories orient recognition and affiliation, while relations help stabilize categories as
socially effective formations (Crossley, 2025a; White, 2008).

The comparison between periods clarifies this point. In the Formation and National
Consolidation period, the network is organised around a visible generational and stylistic

                                                                                                            40
boundary between historical rap and younger urbano-oriented formations. This pattern
resonates with previous research showing that the emergence of Música Urbana in Chile
involved a contested process of categorisation structured by the split between rap and trap,
the development of reggaeton, and the production of generational subjectivities (Muñoz-
Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025). The first-period communities make this boundary
observable at the relational level: rap-oriented clusters are associated with older cohorts and
boom-bap trajectories, while trap, reggaeton, and mambo-oriented clusters are dominated by
younger artists.

In the Boom and International Expansion period, this distinction does not disappear, but it is
reorganised within a larger and more heterogeneous collaboration network. Rap remains
identifiable through specific communities, while larger urbano-oriented clusters are
dominated by artists emerging between 2015 and 2024. At the same time, internationalisation
appears as a selective process rather than a uniform opening of the network. One nucleus is
broader and more industry-visible, combining Chilean artists with collaborators from
multiple international scenes; another is smaller and more specifically connected to Latin
American urbano circuits. This suggests that the expansion of Música Urbana involved both
the persistence of internal generational boundaries and the formation of regional
collaborative horizons.

Taken together, the two periods show that Música Urbana operates as a glocal hypergenre in
a relational sense (Buch, 2013, 2018; Muñoz-Tapia et al., 2025). It gathers heterogeneous
musical lineages without erasing their internal differences. Historical rap, trap, reggaeton,
mambo, and other urbano sounds become part of the same analytical network, yet they
remain differentiated by generation, sonic orientation, collaboration intensity, country
composition, and organisational position. Community detection therefore extends previous
historical and ethnographic research by showing how categorical tensions are also enacted
through collaboration among artists on songs. The next section turns from identifying clusters
to modelling whether selected endogenous processes and artist attributes help account for the
formation of collaboration ties across the two periods.

5.3 ERGM

5.3.1 Binary ERGM Strategy

The final modelling stage uses binary Exponential Random Graph Models (ERGMs) to
examine which mechanisms are associated with the presence of collaboration ties in the
Spotify-visible networks. The dependent network is defined as:

0 = no observed collaboration
1 = at least one observed collaboration

In this analysis, the dependent variable is therefore a binary artist-by-artist collaboration
network estimated separately for each period. A tie takes the value 1 when two artists share
at least one credited track collaboration within the period, and 0 otherwise. This specification
asks a conservative question: given the set of artists retained in each period, which
endogenous and attribute-based mechanisms are associated with whether two artists
collaborate at least once?

Weighted collaboration remains central to the descriptive and community analyses, where tie
intensity is analysed directly. For the ERGM stage, however, the binary specification was
retained because it provided a more stable and comparable strategy across the two periods
than the valued alternatives explored during modelling. The models are estimated separately
for the Formation and National Consolidation period and the Boom and International
Expansion period, following the standard ERGM logic of modelling the observed network
as one realisation from a distribution of possible networks (Koskinen and Snijders, 2012;
Krivitsky, 2012).

The binary ERGM estimates the probability of observing a collaboration network Y as a
function of selected structural and attribute-based statistics:

P(Y = y) = [1 / ()] exp {  g(y) }
where y is the observed collaboration network, g(y) is a vector of network statistics,  is a
vector of model parameters, and () is the normalising constant. In this dissertation, the
model assesses whether collaboration ties are associated with generational proximity, Chile­
foreign ties, categorical similarity, and endogenous network structure.

                                                                                                            42
5.3.2 Model Specification

The model sequence is progressive and intentionally parsimonious. M1 establishes a baseline
for tie formation after accounting for the network's structural design constraints. M2 adds the
two mechanisms most directly connected to the dissertation argument: generational
proximity and Chile-Foreign collaboration. M3 incorporates categorical homophily by
gender and artist type. M4 adds degree(1), an endogenous term that accounts for the large
number of artists with only one observed collaboration partner.

Two structural offsets are included throughout the sequence and are not interpreted
substantively. First, degree(0) is offset because the final analytic networks contain no isolates.
Second, foreign-foreign dyads are offset because foreign or non-core artists enter the data
only through ties to the Chilean Música Urbana core. The Chile-Foreign term therefore
compares Chile-Foreign ties with Chile-Chile ties within the eligible dyad space, rather than
modelling all possible international collaborations.

Because of this limitation, we estimated additional GWDSP specifications as sensitivity

formation period, one GWDSP specification slightly improved AIC but did not improve BIC

relative to M4. In the boom period, analogous specifications were computationally unstable
and did not provide a comparable alternative.

GWDSP is therefore retained only as a sensitivity check. This decision is methodological
rather than substantive: it should not be interpreted as evidence that closure is absent from
the collaboration network. Local cohesion is interpreted primarily through weighted
transitivity and community detection, while the ERGM is used to evaluate more stable
mechanisms of tie formation.

5.3.6 Substantive Interpretation

Because the models are estimated on separate networks, coefficient comparisons across
periods are interpreted cautiously, focusing on direction, consistency, and broad relative
change. The negative edges coefficient in both periods reflects network sparsity: most
eligible dyads do not collaborate. Its more negative value during the boom should not be read
as a decline in collaboration, but as a consequence of the expansion of the dyadic opportunity
structure.

Generational proximity is consistently associated with collaboration. Artists whose
trajectories began closer in time are more likely to collaborate, and this association remains
after adding categorical homophily and low-degree structure. The coefficient is slightly less
negative in the boom period, consistent with a larger network in which more cohorts coexist,
but the effect remains important. This supports the historical interpretation of Chilean Música
Urbana as a field marked by generational differentiation.

Chile-Foreign ties are negative in both periods, with a stronger negative coefficient during
the boom. This does not contradict the descriptive and community results showing increased
internationalisation. Rather, it indicates that cross-national ties remain less likely than Chile-
Chile ties within the eligible dyad space. Internationalisation therefore appears as a selective
expansion concentrated in particular sectors of the network, rather than as a generalised
weakening of national structuring. Community detection identifies where internationalised
collaboration is concentrated, while the ERGM shows that this pattern does not amount to
generalised cross-national mixing across all eligible dyads.

                                                                                                            51
degree(1) is positive and strong in both periods, indicating that artists with only one observed
collaboration partner are not residual cases but part of the network structure. This is
consistent with the descriptive results: the boom expands the network substantially, but many
artists still enter the Spotify-visible collaboration structure through a single observed partner.
Expansion therefore reproduces peripheral modes of participation at a larger scale.

Same-gender collaboration is positive in both periods and stronger in the boom model, but
this result should be interpreted in relation to the strongly masculinised composition of the
network. Since male solo artists constitute the large majority of nodes, same-gender
collaboration partly reflects the field's demographic structure rather than gender operating as
the principal axis of community formation. At the same time, gender is not irrelevant: the
community analysis suggests that the visibility of women and mixed-gender collaboration
depends heavily on specific figures and localised configurations, such as the community
around Ana Tijoux.

Same-artist-type collaboration is also positive in both periods but remains a secondary effect.
The result is consistent with a collaboration field centred mainly on individual artists, while
groups and collectives occupy more specific positions. Finally, the remaining misfit for
edgewise shared partners suggests that local cohesive formations should not be reduced to a
single ERGM closure mechanism. The ERGM therefore complements the descriptive and
community analyses by identifying robust associations with tie formation while preserving a
cautious interpretation of local clustering.

6. Discussion and Conclusion

This dissertation has examined Chilean Música Urbana not simply as a genre label, but as a
historically situated category whose social effectiveness can be observed through patterns of
collaboration. The findings support a relational interpretation of musical categorisation:
categories do not merely describe pre-existing artistic worlds; they also orient recognition,
affiliation, and interaction. In this sense, the analysis extends the argument that categories
and networks are mutually constitutive (Crossley, 2025a; White, 2008). Música Urbana

                                                                                                            52
appears not only as a classificatory device, but as a relational formation through which artists,
scenes, and trajectories become connected.

A central contribution of the study is methodological and interpretive. The network analysis
does not replace historical interpretation; rather, it triangulates and extends it. Muñoz-Tapia
and Pinochet-Cobos (2025) show that the emergence of Música Urbana in Chile involved
transformations in rap, digital self-production, platform visibility, and generational
understandings of what counted as urbano. That historical account is crucial because it gives
specific meaning to the generational divide observed in the network. The distinction between
older rap trajectories and newer urbano clusters is not simply an age difference or a statistical
partition; it corresponds to a locally situated process through which artists, audiences, and
scenes came to distinguish rap, trap, reggaeton, and other urban sounds. The present analysis
extends that account by showing that this categorical and generational divide is also
organised through collaboration ties. In this sense, the relationship between categories and
networks is retroactive: categories orient recognition and affiliation, while repeated
collaborations help reinforce and stabilise those categories. The main contribution, then, lies
in showing how Chilean Música Urbana became relationally organised between formation
and national consolidation and boom and international expansion: it expanded, became more
structurally integrated, and developed through selective internationalisation.

In that sense, this dissertation articulates two analytical emphases that often appear
disconnected: musical genre and art worlds. Genre foregrounds classification, categorisation,
recognition, and symbolic struggle, while art worlds highlight the cooperative chains and
relational infrastructures that make artistic production possible. The Chilean case shows that
these dimensions are not separate: the genre label becomes meaningful through the relations
that enact it, while those relations are oriented by shared categories (Crossley, 2025a; White,
2008). Methodologically, a more quantitative Social Network Analysis can be combined with
qualitative strategies such as interviews and participant observation because each captures a
different dimension of the same cultural process. Historical and ethnographic work clarifies
the category's meanings, while network analysis shows how those meanings become
relationally stabilised through collaboration (Muñoz-Tapia and Pinochet-Cobos, 2025).

                                                                                                            53
This approach is important for cultural network analysis because Spotify collaboration data
are not treated as a transparent representation of a scene. Instead, they serve as an empirical
trace of a broader process of categorisation, circulation, and artistic coordination. The
analysis combines platform data with historical and interpretive knowledge of the Chilean
case, in line with approaches that understand musical categories as historically situated
assemblages rather than fixed stylistic containers (Brackett, 2016; Drott, 2013). Social
Network Analysis therefore makes visible one dimension of the relations through which
categories acquire social force, while accounting for interpretation of the historical and
cultural context.

One of the clearest empirical findings concerns internationalisation. Compared with the
formation and national consolidation period, the boom and international expansion period
includes a larger share of foreign artists, and the community analysis identifies clusters with
stronger international presence. At first glance, this could suggest a general
transnationalisation of Chilean Música Urbana. The ERGM results complicate that
interpretation. Chile-Foreign ties remain less likely than Chile-Chile ties, and this negative
association is stronger during the second period. Internationalisation should therefore not be
understood as a homogeneous opening of the entire collaboration network. Rather, it appears
as a selective process concentrated in particular relational sectors. Foreign artists become
more visible and more numerous, but cross-national collaboration does not become a
generalised pattern across the eligible dyad space.

This result speaks to broader debates on streaming and global music circulation.
Hesmondhalgh (2025) argues that streaming has produced more multipolar patterns of
musical visibility while preserving unequal structures of platform ownership, infrastructural
control, and market power. Chilean Música Urbana fits this ambivalent landscape. The boom
period allowed local artists to connect with broader Latin American circuits, but this
expansion remained selective and uneven. The findings therefore nuance celebratory
accounts of platform globalisation: streaming may increase transnational visibility, but it
does not automatically produce evenly distributed transnational collaboration.

Network expansion also did not produce symmetric integration. Between the two periods,
the network shows a sharp increase in nodes, edges, and total collaborations, and the largest

                                                                                                            54
component contains a greater share of the network. In this sense, the collaboration network
became more structurally integrated. Yet this integration coexists with persistent inequality
in participation. In terms of collaboration opportunities, expansion increased the number of
possible positions in the network without distributing access to collaboration evenly across
artists. Most artists continue to have few collaborators, and the ERGM shows a strong
positive degree(1) effect in both periods. The second period therefore did not eliminate
peripheral participation; it reproduced it at a larger scale.

This pattern is especially relevant in the context of the featuring economy and platform
visibility. Collaboration can function as a mechanism of circulation, exposure, and
legitimacy, but a single collaboration does not necessarily imply stable integration into a
collaborative world. The network grows through both one-off ties and recurrent partnerships,
producing a structure that is simultaneously larger, more integrated, and highly
heterogeneous. The transition between periods should therefore be understood not only as
growth, but also as uneven relational incorporation.

Gender appears as a secondary but important dimension of the analysis. The descriptive
results show that the network is strongly masculinised in both periods. The ERGM identifies
a positive same-gender effect, particularly during the boom, but this coefficient must be
interpreted cautiously because male solo artists constitute the overwhelming majority of
nodes. Gender homophily partly reflects the field's demographic structure. The community
analysis further suggests that gender does not organise the main divisions of the network,
which are more clearly differentiated by generation, musical trajectory, country composition,
and international orientation. However, gender is not irrelevant. The visibility of women and
mixed-gender collaboration appears to depend heavily on specific figures and localised
configurations. The community around Ana Tijoux is especially important in this respect,
since it connects historical rap trajectories with women artists, foreign artists, and artists
located partly outside the core of Música Urbana. Gender therefore operates less as a global
organising principle than as an uneven and localised dimension of collaboration.

The study also has methodological implications for analysing cultural categories through
networks. Descriptive measures show expansion and integration, community detection
identifies meso-level clusters, and the ERGM evaluates mechanisms associated with tie

                                                                                                            55
formation. No single method captures the full process. This is especially clear for local
cohesion: weighted transitivity and community detection indicate cohesive local formations,
while the ERGM does not fully reproduce edgewise shared partners. Collaboration clusters
therefore require both formal modelling and historical-cultural interpretation.

The approach also clarifies the limits of Spotify-visible data. The dataset captures
collaborations that are visible on the platform, not the entire set of musical, social, and
organisational relations that constitute the scene. It therefore offers a partial but analytically
useful trace of collaboration. By combining these data with historical knowledge and
manually validated artist attributes, the dissertation avoids treating platform data as self-
sufficient evidence.

Taken together, the findings show that the formation, consolidation, and boom of Chilean
Música Urbana cannot be understood only as a story of stylistic change, platform visibility,
or international success. It is also a process of relational transformation. The category gathers
heterogeneous musical trajectories, but it does not erase their internal differences.
Generational boundaries, selective internationalisation, uneven participation, and localised
gender visibility all remain visible within the collaboration network. The dissertation
therefore contributes to a cultural-relational understanding of musical categories: categories
acquire social force not only through discourse, markets, or platforms, but through the
patterned relations that connect artists and make a musical world observable across time.

6.1 Methodological Limitations

The networks analysed in this dissertation represent collaborations that are visible in Spotify
metadata, not the complete universe of musical relations. They capture credited co-
participation on released tracks but exclude informal creative exchanges, uncredited
production and songwriting, live performance, friendship, management, and other relations
that may shape collaboration. The network boundary is therefore substantive and platform-
specific rather than a census of all artists or interactions in Chilean music.

The sharp increase between periods should also be interpreted as a combination of scene
expansion and changing platform visibility. More artists may have entered the field, but
digital distribution, access to Spotify profiles, metadata coverage, and formal crediting

                                                                                                            56
practices also expanded. The later network consequently documents the growth of the
Spotify-visible collaboration field rather than providing a direct estimate of growth in the
underlying population of musicians.

Artist classification combines Spotify genre metadata with manual validation. This improves
the situated validity of the Música Urbana category, but it also introduces interpretive
decisions that cannot be reduced to a platform label. The relational boundary mitigates this
issue by requiring external artists to connect directly to the selected Chilean Música Urbana
network, and the audit confirms that every final edge satisfies this rule.
